Water meter still running or a high water bill.

The simplest test is free. Turn off every fixture and appliance, then look at your water meter. If the small dial or low-flow indicator still moves, water is escaping somewhere between the meter and your fixtures.

Next, shut the main valve at the house. If the meter stops, the leak is inside the house. If it keeps running, the leak is in the service line between the meter and the foundation. That one test narrows a search from the whole property to a single section of pipe.

A bill that creeps up over a few months without any change in habits usually points to a toilet flapper, a slow supply leak or a failing buried line. We compare your meter readings against the last billing period to size the loss before we begin.

Signs of a hidden leak.

You do not need to hear dripping to have a leak. These are the clues we hear about most.

  • A water meter that moves when every tap is closed..
  • A warm spot on a floor, or a hot-water line that is warm when no one is using it..
  • The sound of running water in a quiet house at night..
  • A damp, musty smell in a closet, cabinet or crawlspace..
  • A brown ring on a ceiling, bubbling paint or wallpaper that peels at the seam..
  • A patch of lawn that stays green and soggy between the meter and the house..

Slab leaks: when the pipe is under the concrete.

A slab leak is a break in a supply line that runs beneath a concrete floor. Homes in Chillicothe with slab-on-grade additions or garage conversions are the likeliest candidates. The usual clues are a warm patch of floor, a higher gas bill from a water heater that runs constantly and a meter that moves with nothing on.

We pressure-test the hot and cold systems separately to learn which side is losing pressure, then use a ground microphone and a tracer gas or thermal tool to pin down the spot before anything is cut.

Locating the break to within a few feet matters, because the choice between a spot repair, a reroute through the attic and a full repipe depends on how many other lines are failing.

Ceiling, wall and attic leaks.

Water that drips through a ceiling is rarely directly under the leak. It runs along a joist or pipe and surfaces a few feet away. Cutting at the stain often opens the wrong spot.

We use moisture mapping to follow the wet area back to the source. A tub overflow gasket, a failed shower pan, a loose supply fitting or a roof leak can all produce the same stain. We tell you honestly if the cause is plumbing or roofing before you pay to open anything.

If a ceiling is bowed and heavy with water, poke a small hole in the lowest point and let it drain into a bucket. A sagging ceiling that is left alone can fall.

Crawlspace, under-house and buried-line leaks.

Under-house leaks are easier to reach than slab leaks but harder to notice. Insulation hangs wet, wood darkens and the floor above feels soft. We inspect the crawlspace with a moisture meter and trace each supply line.

A leak in the buried line between the meter and the house shows up as a sinking patch of ground, a wet spot in the yard or a meter that runs with the house valve closed. Before any digging we mark the location and call Ohio811, which requires notice at least two working days before excavation. Homeowners can request locates through 811 or at OHIO811.org.

Irrigation lines, meter boxes and hose bibs are the other usual outdoor suspects, and we check each one in the same visit.

Leaks and the Chillicothe ground.

Chillicothe was built where the Scioto River meets Paint Creek, and the 1913 flood put about 70 percent of the city underwater. Levees along the river now protect the city, but wet soil and a high water table are still facts of life here. A buried line that leaks in saturated ground may not surface, which is why a meter test is more reliable than looking for a puddle.

In winter, soil movement and freezing add stress to old service lines. If a leak shows up after a hard freeze, mention it when you call, because it changes where we start looking.

What affects leak detection pricing.

We explain the scope before we begin so you can decide how far to go.

  • Access. An open crawlspace is faster than a finished basement ceiling.
  • Number of systems tested. Hot, cold and outdoor lines each take time.
  • Property size and age. Larger or older plumbing layouts take longer to isolate.
  • Repair scope. Finding the leak and fixing it are priced separately, and you can use a different contractor for the repair.

Insurers often ask for proof of where and when a leak occurred. Our report names the location and the method used, which helps with a claim. Ask your insurer whether detection costs are covered under your policy.

Methods and limits of leak finding.

Leak detection uses published physics, not guesswork: a pressure test isolates the line, acoustic listening picks up the sound of escaping water and a meter test confirms flow. None of these is a code standard, so we state their limits plainly. A line under concrete can usually be narrowed to a small area, and the repair still follows the Ohio Plumbing Code, which is built on the International Plumbing Code (IPC).
